## Design Workbook

This is a long assignment description: you'll be doing the assignment every week all semester so please read it thoroughly, and if you're having trouble with the assignment return to it often.

### Goals

The goal of this recurring assignment is to develop your ability to use early design concepts (1) to exemplify and explore possible societal impacts of design and (2) to develop your own voice on class topics. You will keep a paper notebook all through the semester for lecture notes, and assignment planning and iteration.

After doing this assignment you will be able to:

1.  Identify and create a personal response to a specific idea in a reading
2.  Use speculative design to (a) explore social implications of design and (b) ideate new design possibilities
3.  Construct a compelling argument through design
4.  Use design as a means of discovery and reflection

### Background

Designers tend to go through many drafts before making a final design, and the drafts are often much about iterating **what the problem really is** as they are about working on the solution. Most designers use some form of workbook as a way to think through those the problem they are working on. So first and foremost, please be clear that design workbooks are real-world tools, not just for students of design, and how they are used varies a lot by designer. After this class you will spend the rest of your life figuring out how workbooks might help you across different projects. But in this class, the design workbooks are **weekly speculative designs done in response to course readings**. Because this class focuses on understanding design at a high level, we're actually looking for your design's **interaction with the readings more than for a great idea for a new product or service**. Think of it as the equivalent to a "reading response" in a humanities course, where students use the response to think about and reflect on the reading. We want you to show us that you understand the reading enough to design with/against it, so the main criteria for a "good" design workbook submission is how well it is in dialog with the reading.

More generically, outside of this class, **design workbooks** are commonly used in the early (research, ideate, sketch, repeat) stage of a design. They are a method for exploring the parameters of the design's context, addressing specific issues or premises that underpin the design, or playing with materials or techniques that might be included in the design. Particularly in the early stages of design, design workbooks are explicitly **not intended to provide a straightforward answer to a design problem**, but instead explore trade-offs and possible directions that the final design might foreclose upon or ellide. Please reread the last sentence, because it is critical to the entire semester. Non-designers think that designers are brought in to solve a problem: designers in fact have to figure out what the problem really is, and usually not just how to solve it but how to go about solving it. That is to say, a design workbook at this stage **does not contain practical solutions that a designer would expect to be implemented, but rather "exploratory" designs that help them to understand the nature of the problem** they are facing. The design is often annotated with brief explanations for communicating to others, or to one's self later when one might be in a different headspace. These annotated designs may also tentatively explore the possible social worlds ("life worlds"), new experiences, or implications opened up by the design.

### Our workbooks
#### What we mean when we say Design Workbook
In this course, when we say "design workbooks," most of the time we're referring to the actual notebook you'll be using all semester, or the short (one page) PDF submissions you'll be making to Canvas every week. They can take many forms, and we encourage you to experiment a bit with format and style throughout the semester. We don't show examples of prior students' workbooks, because we generally love individual and adventurous submissions. Can't draw? That doesn't  matter. You can trace, collage, do [xkcd](https://xkcd.com/2598/) style kid's drawings. Do your best to communicate, we'll meet you half way, though we do hope you'll develop your designerly communications skills as you go along.

So we mostly mean 1) "your weekly assignment" when we say "design workbooks," but we may also be referring to 2) the specific physical workbook that *you* keep as a way to take lecture notes on the readings and generate your assignments, or 3) the larger method of workbooks, as used by Bill Gaver and other designers we discuss in class. Pay attention to context and you won't be confused.

Though Canvas submissions of the final workbook are PDF, you will use your notebook for the entire class. It's important to make notes, drawings, and scribbles *while* you are listening to lectures and reading. It's far more easy to be inspired while listening or reading, or right after, than to do so days later. If you are inspired directly while reading, or right after reading, your work will often be good enough to be submitted right away. In other cases, though, you may want to refine, edit, or redraw/rewrite a bit before submitting. We will scan your physical workbook on some Fridays.

#### How to ace your workbooks
For the actual submissions, we use design workbooks as **a method for exploring social issues in and through design** and for **responding to authors' arguments and creating our own arguments**. The workbooks are a designerly form of reading response: how can I use a design to respond to topics and issues that arise in a reading? This is not Shark Tank, you're not pitching any new product. It's a product where, in effect, the course is the user, and its engagement with ideas of the readings define how usable/sticky the product is.

**The form of your design workbook is open**. The submissions will not be judged negatively on artistic execution, but rather on the sophistication and clarity with which they explore class-related issues, their accuracy in connecting with arguments and ideas in class readings, and their creativity and variety in concept. People who can't draw *can and do* ace workbook pages. You may execute your workbook by hand, electronically, or in some combination of both; you may use photographs, hand-drawn images, collages from found images, or some other form; you may vary the ratio of text and image as makes sense for your own practice and understanding. It is OK to sketch using very poor drawing skills (this is what one of the professors does herself). We do, however, encourage you to try different visual techniques for some or all of your workbooks: why not experiment? Some students find one method and do it all semester. We don't downgrade them for it, but are trying to learn about design, or hoping to work on an unchanging assembly line?

### What you will do
You will use your workbook for everything in the course: taking lecture notes, reading notes, and drafting your design workbook. On Fridays you'll scan your workbook in section to capture your reading, lecture notes, and drafts, then on Tuesdays you'll submit a pdf with your final workbook. Each Tuesday submission will have **a single design** and two quick variations of a [product, service, device, infrastructure, space, etc.], which are anchored in a single, specific idea or issue which you have identified from a single course reading. One design is due for each week, using one of the readings for that that week, unless otherwise specified on the course schedule.

Example: Week X has a Tuesday and Thursday lecture, so the following Monday a workbook will be due that covers ONE of the readings from those lectures, chosen from the readings specified in the assignment on the course schedule page. Each assignment on the course schedule specifies which single reading can be used that week.


The components of a workbook:
1.  **Quote and Recap** Choose a specific passage from the reading (include the page number!), an important or evocative idea passage that will inspire your design, or that the design is commenting on. This may be 1-4 sentences. [Here is an example of how to cite.]({{ site.url }}{{ site.baseurl }}/my_assets/img/DW_Idea_format_annotated.png) <-- Follow this example (at least at first) to be sure you submit correctly. Then write from one to a few sentences describing the specific idea of the author's on which you are basing the design in **your own words**. Ideally, you then show how that specific idea works relates to one or two other main ideas from the reading. All this helps us to know that you understood the reading. There is a limit to the entire section (including quote) of 120 words.

2.  **Draw/Explain and Annotate a Design** Your design can be up to a few sentences, and/or a drawing annotated with short statements (they do not need to be full sentences). The annotations should **reflect on issues that the design raises** and how they **relate to the idea from the reading.** These annotations should **NOT** argue for the value of your design; we're not venture capital looking to buy. Instead, use the design as a way to explore the idea from the reading: what possibilities would exist if your design were real? These annotations should be *nuanced* (i.e., not present a black-and-white view of the issues, of the possibilities of your design, or of the reading) and accurately represent the author's argument, even (especially) when you disagree with it. Your design is ideally new and original, but it *has to spring from the author's context.* Not sure if it's original? Do a search to see if it exists already.

    Note that your design doesn't have to be practical; you don't have to explain exactly how it works; it doesn't need to be buildable in the fall of {{ site.data.yearly.Year }} (but should be plausible in the next year or two); it doesn't need a market; you don't even have to want to own it or use it. That said, it shouldn't be magic. A good design will seem like something that could exist now or soon. No magic, though. A pill that makes you a genius; a pump that turns garbage into delicious healthy smoothies; an electronic collar that prevents traffic accidents; an AI that just churns out the correct answer: these are *magic, not technology*. You need to make something that actually does the work a technology does, not a magic device that does an endrun directly to the solution. If you have trouble with this idea, try to go to a simple technology: what is a literal lever, algorithm, human labor, or physical machine that can address the issue? And never forget that *its ultimate function is really to engage with the readings.* 

3. **Consider Two Other Designs You Could Have Done, Once Sentence Each.** Good designers aren't too precious with     their early ideas. Indeed, each design should spawn other ideas that you might or might not want to explore, but you nonetheless should document. After your in-depth design, imagine two more designs that are different approaches, but still generated in dialog with the reading. Try to mix it up: if your first design was phone-sized, make another house-sized and the next a software platform. If the first design agreed with the author's point, try one that didn't agree. We suggest that you adopt a sentence like: "An X that does Y [and maybe Z],[tie to the reading]." One sentence each isn't much, so take care to write it pithily. Here's an example of two designs responding to Scott's text of the High Modernist city, which describes Lúcio Costa and Oscar Niemeyer's de novo design of the city of Brazilia, and the many unanticipated problems that resulted from that design:

    "1) An art exhibition that stochastically rearranges its layout and wall text, allowing viewers to take away new meanings and relationships to betweek art works across multiple visits; on the other hand sometimes the connections don't help understanding, and lead to more wear and tear on the art. 2) A tightly run double-blind city zoning commission, like a jury, that isn't allowed to know anything about landlords, politicians, or developers, to avoid untoward influence." 
    
    Neither of these designs is groundbreaking, but they were fun to come up with, and they certainly demonstrate that I read the Scott and understood it.

4.  **What Did You Learn About the Author's Points By Making the Designs?** This final stage is one that may take a few of the semester to fully understand. Central to design practice is that you learn about problems and their solutions by designing. Your regular design workbook assignments are about you using your designs to not only regurtitate the readings, but to add to them by using your designs to think with.

While this is the last part of the assignment, once you've tried it a few times, you'll realize that you need to do stages 1-3 in such a way that you have the right raw material to work with for stage 4. If you chose a straightforward or didactic point from the reading, and you created a design that simply illustrates that point in the reading, and squeezed out two mearly illustrative designs, you probably haven't learned anything from doing the design. Really, you want to have chosen a complex point from the reading, found a thoughtful way to engage with it, then done two very different designs. At that point, more likely than not, you'll have learned something new about the author's point. As always, this final part of the assignment needs to be in dialog with the reading, but uses your designs and process to synthesize your own new point.

5. **Summary**

What we are looking for in this design is 1) an accurate reflection of a specific idea from the author of a reading, 2) a creative design idea clearly inspired by or reflecting on that idea, 3) a couple of other designs that show you're generating more ideas, and 4) thoughtful and personal response that uses your design to reflect and build on the reading in interesting ways.

What we are _not_ looking for in this assignment is a "good design" for the consumer market with a justification for why the design is good. It is not unusual for your workbook designs to have serious problems, which your annotations can and should explore. The design might reflect limitations of, alternatives to, or questions about the issues raised in the reading, but again they must be clearly tied to the reading. Again: we want you to be able to show that you understand an author's ideas, and to design in dialog with those ideas. 

Why? Why do reading responses through design? In lecture we show examples of how often designers can be trapped in their own values, and copy those values into their designs without knowing they are doing so. Doing a semester of design exercises based on other people's arguements helps you to reflect on values that aren't your own, and to work them into design intentionally. This *detachment* builds your ability to be conscious of how values are built into designs, and to better make your own *fully conscious* design arguments later.

Again, your submissions will not be judged negatively on poor artistic execution or sentence fragments, but on the sophistication and clarity with which they explore course-related issues, their accuracy in connecting with an author's arguments and ideas in class readings, and their creativity. That said, graders will absolutely note effort, care, and excellent execution, and they love clear and compelling submissions. No one has been downgraded for a very well-thought-out, readable, and unique submission, and indeed it can be the difference between an 18 and a 20.

### Incremental Submissions for the first four weeks

For each of your **first four submissions,** you'll add one part of the four parts listed above successivly. So in the first week, as only one lecture has readings due, your ONE design workbook will ONLY consist of part 1: **Quote and Recap**. The second week, your two workbooks will include both parts 1 and 2: **Quote and Recap** AND **Draw/Explain and Annotate a Design**. The fourth submission, your workbooks will have ALL FOUR PARTS of a complete workbook, outlined above. All workbooks will be graded EXTREMELY GENEROUSLY until the fifth submission, **but with comments.** It is your job to read those comments and try to improve. The comments may even indicate what your score would be after week four, if you don't take the comments into account and improve.

### Grading

Each page in your workbook will be graded out of 25 points, using the following measures:

*   **Notes and Drafts (5 points)**: We look at your lecture notes and drafts, submitted during the Friday section, judged on completeness, playfulness, and evidence of effort.
*   **Idea and Recap (4 points)**: A single, specific idea from an identified, appropriately chosen reading is clearly stated and correctly described.
*   **Design (4 points)**: An original design (Google it!) that responds clearly to the idea. The design does not need to be 'good' as a design, taken alone.
*   **Other Designs (4 points)**: A clear, nuanced, convincing analysis uses the possibilities opened up by the design to reflect on the reading in a clearly articulated way.
*   **Reflection (4 points)**: What did you learn from doing your designs?
*   **Effort and Originality (2 points, extra credit)**: Effort has been put into the assignment. You've stretched yourself in how you communicate.

An assignment that meets all these criteria will receive an A (22.5/25). Additional credit is possible at the discretion of the TAs for designs that are truly imaginative, as well as for reflections that are truly thought-provoking and original. Extra points are unusual.

### Critiques

Twice per semester we run critiques in discussion section. We'll explain critiques in lecture, but they are essentially a way to discuss, compare, contrast, and seek help on your workbooks. You'll be expected to put paper copies of your workbooks on the classroom wall. You'll receive feedback on your work, and give feedback to your peers.
